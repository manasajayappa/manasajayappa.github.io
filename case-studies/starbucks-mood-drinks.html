The Problem

Most customers feel decision fatigue before they even order

"There are 170,000+ possible drink combinations at Starbucks. For many customers, that's not freedom — it's paralysis."

The Starbucks app is optimized for speed of re-order, but it doesn't help customers discover what they actually want on a given day. A customer who's exhausted at 2 PM has completely different needs than the same customer starting a creative work session at 9 AM — yet the app presents them with the same static menu both times.

Personalization today is habit-driven: the app learns your order history and surfaces your frequent picks. But mood is transient. A great recommendation requires knowing how you feel right now — not just what you ordered last Tuesday.

Discovery

What we learned from 24 interviews and 400 survey responses

I ran a five-week discovery sprint with a UX researcher and a data analyst. We conducted in-depth interviews with frequent app users (3+ visits/week) and ran a targeted survey across loyalty tier segments. Three clear user types emerged around mood-ordering behavior.

The Energy Seeker

Maya, 28

Marketing coordinator who visits before work and at the 2 PM slump. Needs reliable caffeine but craves variety to avoid "coffee boredom."

"I know I want something that'll wake me up but I keep ordering the same thing because I don't know what else to try."

The Mindful Sipper

Alex, 34

Remote UX designer who visits midday. Prioritizes wellness and avoids excess caffeine. Feels the menu isn't curated for their needs.

"I want something calming but the app just pushes espresso drinks at me."

The Occasion Drinker

Jordan, 22

College student who treats Starbucks as a social ritual and mood signal. Posts drinks on social media. Wants new, shareable combinations.

"I want something that feels like it matches my energy today, not just whatever is on the seasonal menu."

Key insight: Across all three personas, mood is an active input in the drink decision. Users have intuitive language for how they feel ("drained," "antsy," "cozy") but the app gives them no surface to translate that language into a recommendation.

The Feature

Mood-matched drink recommendations, surfaced at the right moment

At the top of the app's home screen, a soft prompt: "How are you feeling today?" The user selects a mood from six curated states. The feature surfaces three drink recommendations tailored to that mood and their order history — with a clear explanation of why each drink fits.

Roadmap

From concept to beta in three phases

The delivery strategy staggers complexity: ship a useful v1 fast, learn from real usage, and build toward ML personalization only once we have enough mood-order signal to train on.

Q2 2026
Phase 1 — Foundation (v1.0)
  • Finalize mood taxonomy with UX and beverage team
  • Build static rules-based recommendation engine
  • Design and implement mood selector UI on home screen
  • Write "Why this drink" copy for all 18 top recommendations
  • Internal alpha: 50 Starbucks employees
Q3 2026
Phase 2 — Personalization (v1.1)
  • Integrate order history to filter recs by dietary preference
  • Add time-of-day context layer to mood surfacing logic
  • A/B test mood selector placement (home screen vs. cart page)
  • Limited beta: 25,000 Starbucks Rewards members in 3 cities
  • Collect mood → order → satisfaction feedback signal
Q4 2026
Phase 3 — Intelligence (v2.0)
  • Train collaborative filtering model on mood + order + satisfaction data
  • Replace static rules with ML-driven recommendations
  • Add social sharing (mood card → Instagram Stories)
  • Explore seasonal mood variants for holiday states
  • Nationwide rollout to all Starbucks app users

PM Reflection

What this project taught me

1

Emotion is a first-class input — build for it intentionally

Most product recommendations are behavior-driven: "people who bought X also bought Y." Mood-driven UX requires a different model — one where the input is ephemeral and self-reported. This means the design of the input (how you ask the question) is as critical as the algorithm behind the output.

2

Ship a rules-based v1 faster than you think you need to

I initially pushed for building an ML model from the start. Our data scientist correctly argued that we didn't yet have the mood-labeled order data to train on. A static rules-based approach gave us something in front of users in weeks, and the signal we collected from real behavior became the training dataset for v2.

3

The explanation is part of the product

Early prototypes without the "Why this drink" copy tested poorly — users felt recommendations were arbitrary. Adding one sentence of ingredient rationale ("matcha's L-theanine promotes calm focus") dramatically improved trust scores. Copy is a product feature, not an afterthought.

4

Mood taxonomy is a design decision, not a data decision

I ran two workshops to align on the six mood states. An early draft had twelve — too many, and they started overlapping. Narrowing to six required prioritizing moods with the clearest drink differentiation, which forced the beverage team and design team to align on shared language. That alignment was more valuable than any individual feature.
